Education Department tackles ‘pass the trash’ in K-12 schools

The Trump administration is taking on the practice of teachers accused of allegations being allowed to move to other schools or districts

July 27, 2026 A.P. Dillon Article, News

RALEIGH — The U.S. Department of Education announced an initiative to hold accountable states and districts engaging in a practice known as “pass the trash.”

Pass the trash is when a teacher is allowed to keep teaching or teachers are permitted to transfer to another school or district despite allegations of sexual misconduct with students.

“Our schools must protect America’s children. Parents should never have to wonder whether their kids’ school employs and protects sexual predators,” U.S. Secretary of Education Linda McMahon said in a press release.

“Schools that receive federal funding have a duty to protect students, report sexual misconduct honestly, and follow the law. Through this initiative, we are holding school districts accountable because every child deserves to learn in a safe environment free from sexual abuse and harassment.”

According to the press release, the Education Department’s Office of Civil Rights has opened 20 investigations into districts based on 2023-24 Civil Rights Data Collection (CRDC) submissions where staff conduct may not have been appropriately addressed.

The Education Department provided the list of districts to North State Journal: Arizona — Fredonia-Moccasin Unified District; California — Tulare City, Wilsona; Connecticut — Connecticut Technical Education and Career System; Georgia — DeKalb County, Richmond County, Savannah-Chatham County; Kentucky — Perry County; Michigan — Detroit Public Schools Community District, Pontiac City School District, Taylor School District; Missouri — Springfield R-XII; New Hampshire — Manchester School District; New Jersey — Gloucester Township Public Schools; Pennsylvania — Cheltenham School District; South Carolina — Marion 10; Tennessee — Marion County; Texas — North East ISD; Virginia — Campbell County Public Schools; Washington — Battle Ground School District.

McMahon’s press release included a Dear Colleague letter sent to K-12 institutions receiving federal funding, outlining “legal obligations to safeguard children and appropriately respond to incidents of sexual misconduct under the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (ESEA) and Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 (Title IX).”

The legal obligations pertain to the “Prohibition on Aiding and Abetting Sexual Abuse,” which is a funding condition enacted in 2015 as part of the Every Student Succeeds Act.

Research cited in the letter says, “In 2003, it was estimated that 9.6 percent of 8th to 11th grade students had reported sexual misconduct by their educators.”

It is unclear if the data cited in the letter is the same as a Department of Justice commissioned report released in 2017, which revealed “an estimated 10% of K–12 students will experience sexual misconduct by a school employee by the time they graduate from high school.”

The Dear Colleague letter also underscored that districts need to ensure they are collecting and submitting accurate CRDC data or face penalties.

North State Journal requested comment on the letter from the North Carolina State Board of Education. A communications staffer with the N.C. Department of Public Instruction (NCDPI) said they received the letter and “will be reviewing the federal request across our leadership team early next week.”

The NCDPI staffer also said the department will be “sending guidance” for all districts. NCDPI issued the following additional statement regarding the Dear Colleague letter:

The North Carolina Department of Public Instruction has reviewed the July 10, 2026 “Dear Colleague” letter from the U.S. Department of Education. Both NCDPI and the State Board of Education take seriously their obligation to protect students from sexual misconduct by school employees.

North Carolina state law requires public school administrators to report licensed professional educators who have been charged with or convicted of certain serious criminal acts or who have engaged in conduct involving the physical abuse of a child to the State Board of Education within five days of the educator’s dismissal, resignation, or receipt of a disciplinary employment action. The failure to do so carries felony penalties. In addition, State Board of Education policy requires public school administrators to report other forms of misconduct by professional educators to the State Board within 30 days of the educator’s dismissal, resignation, or receipt of disciplinary employment action. NCDPI is not aware of any school districts or charter schools that are out of compliance with this law.

When the State Board receives a report or complaint involving alleged sexual misconduct by a licensed educator, the State Board opens a state-level investigation. Depending on the outcome, the Superintendent of Public Instruction can move to suspend or revoke an educator’s license. Given that the suspension or revocation of a license implicates a property interest of the educator (the license), the educator is entitled to due process before that license is taken away. As such, licensed educators have the right to request a formal hearing in the Office of Administrative Hearings before final action is taken.

NCDPI reports all public disciplinary actions (including formal reprimands, suspensions, and revocations) on its website. North Carolina also participates in the NASDTEC Educator Identification Clearinghouse, which flags licensed educators subjected to disciplinary actions by the State Board of Education to other states where those individuals may also be licensed or have applied for a license.

NCDPI respectfully disagrees with your assertion that the teacher license discipline data is out of date . As of July 22, 2026, that page shows 22 actions entered in 2026, including three license revocations. Keep in mind that a state disciplinary action is not final and is not entered in the database until the State Board has completed its investigation and the educator in question has exhausted any available due process procedures. As such, there is often a delay between the opening of an investigation and when disciplinary action is made public.

NCDPI will continue working to ensure North Carolina’s laws and processes for reporting and disciplining educator misconduct are followed and enforced.

Unaddressed in McMahon’s announcement is the patchwork of background-check systems for K-12 teachers, with different systems often used from state to state and even between districts within a state — including North Carolina, which received an “F” for screening teachers by USA Today in 2016.

North Carolina has several laws that aim to address sexual abuse of students and to end pass the trash.

During the 2015-16 legislative session, the Protect Our Students Act (House Bill 113) was signed into law. That bill raised penalties for sex acts involving students from misdemeanors to felonies.

In 2017, laws around teacher resignations were tightened, requiring district officials to report to the State Board of Education whenever a teacher resigned without the required approval from a superintendent. That statute, however, had no enforcement teeth.

Former State Superintendent Catherine Truitt ran an agency bill in 2023, called The Protect Our Students Act (House Bill 142), which changed the penalty for crimes of sexual activity with a student and taking indecent liberties with a student to Class G felony instead of Class I.

The bill also addressed grooming tactics by predators and strengthened the 2017 resignations reporting law, making it a Class I felony for failing to report teacher misconduct to the State Board of Education.

In 2024, the State Board of Education took up proposals to tighten up language on teacher license disciplinary actions, including giving the state superintendent more latitude in suspending or revoking a license for a teacher accused of serious misconduct.

North Carolina is not immune to pass the trash tactics.

Between 2016 and 2024, there were 456 teacher arrests logged in North Carolina, with some cases involving a pass the trash situation.

There are likely more cases, as the data was self-compiled from court records, arrest reports, tips and media reporting. North Carolina currently has no tracking mechanism for these types of crimes.

Of the arrests logged, 320 involved a sex crime, typically involving a student or multiple students. Out of the 456, 423 were employed by a public school and 26 by a private school, and 256 held a valid state teaching license at the time of arrest.

While many cases are still unresolved in the courts, data so far shows 254 convictions and 86 required to register as a sex offender.
